Dan Luu recently did something almost nobody bothers to do. He sat down and [graded a famous AI skeptic's predictions](https://danluu.com/zitron/) against what actually happened. The skeptic, Ed Zitron, had spent two years calling peak AI, forecasting collapses at Meta and Google, and putting dates on when the bubble would pop. The scoreboard was brutal. Nearly every specific, checkable call was wrong.

It would be easy to read that as a win for the AI optimists. It isn't. The crowd promising AGI on a fixed date and agents replacing whole departments this year has watched its own timelines slip just as often. Both camps are selling the same product, and it is the one thing a leader running a real budget cannot use: certainty.

## Confidence is the product

A calibrated take does not travel. "Maybe, and here is the sixty-forty" gets no clicks, sells no books, wins no arguments on a podcast. Anger and certainty travel. So the loudest voices in your feed are selected for confidence, not accuracy, and the selection gets stronger the more the take spreads.

Your job as a decision-maker is the reverse of a pundit's. You need the probability, the causal chain, and the thing that would change your mind. A viral, self-assured prediction carries almost none of that by design. One pattern I have noticed advising teams: the more emphatically someone states an AI timeline, the less they can tell you about what would falsify it. Treat confidence as a discount, not a signal.

## A right answer is worthless if the reasoning is wrong

Here is the part that matters more than the score. Luu's real finding was not that Zitron was wrong. Plenty of people are wrong. It was that he was wrong for reasons that do not hold up, leaning on third-party traffic estimates over companies' own reported numbers, blaming individual executives for structural decisions.

That distinction is everything for a decision-maker. A prediction is an output. What you actually need is the model that produced it, because next quarter you have to run that model again on a different question. A skeptic who lands a call by luck and a booster who guesses the right date are equally useless to you, because you cannot reuse the reasoning. Grade the mechanism, not the outcome. The forecaster worth listening to is the one who can show you the machinery and tell you where it breaks.

## Keep your own scoreboard

What Luu did to a public pundit, every leadership team should do to its own roadmap, and almost none do. It costs nothing and it is the highest-leverage habit in AI strategy.

Write down, with a date, what you expect from a bet before you make it. The support pilot will cut handle time by a fifth. Adoption will reach half the team within a quarter. The model will hold its accuracy on our own tickets, not a public benchmark. Then check. Most companies never write the expectation down, so when a bet underperforms they cannot tell whether the bet was bad or the process around it was, and they argue about it instead of learning from it.

The teams getting real value already do a version of this one level down: they build [a small evaluation from their own merged work](/2026/your-backlog-is-the-benchmark) rather than trusting a leaderboard. The same discipline scales up to the strategy itself. The advantage is speed, a short loop between what you expected and what actually happened. That loop, [how fast the company turns evidence into a changed decision](/2026/how-fast-your-company-learns), is the thing competitors cannot copy off a slide.

## Stop trying to win the argument

You will not settle the hype-versus-doom debate before you have to act, and the good news is you do not need to. The framing is a trap. The useful axis is not optimist versus pessimist. It is reversible versus irreversible.

Amazon's Jeff Bezos [splits decisions into one-way and two-way doors](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/000119312516530910/d168744dex991.htm): the irreversible ones deserve slow, careful debate, and the reversible ones should be made fast and unwound if they fail. Most AI bets can be built as two-way doors. A scoped pilot, cheap to run, priced so the downside is a known number, on [dull and predictable infrastructure](/2026/boring-technology-is-an-ai-strategy) you already understand. When a decision is reversible, you do not need to be right about the trajectory of the entire field. You only need it to be cheap to be wrong. That is what an MVP is for in the first place, not to prove the technology works, but to shrink the range of things you are uncertain about for the least money.

The uncomfortable part is that keeping score makes you accountable. A pundit never has to face a scoreboard, which is the whole luxury of selling certainty to other people. A leader does. If I were advising a team drowning in AI takes, I would spend zero minutes deciding which forecaster to believe and all of them building the ledger. The market has never rewarded the company that guessed the timeline. It rewards the one that noticed fastest which of its own bets were working.
