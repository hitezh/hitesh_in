A broker will sell you a hundred thousand dollars a day of frontier AI, routed through their own endpoint, at roughly 40 percent off list. Smaller lots trade on named storefronts, AICreditMart and Tokvana and Neokens and CheapCredits among them, at anywhere from 30 to 80 percent off. The person who [traced this market](https://vectoral.com/blog/who-are-the-token-brokers) reckons tens of millions of dollars of credits are moving through it: some resold from unused startup grants, some bought with stolen cards, some of no clear origin at all.

The easy reading is fraud, and there is real fraud in it. The more useful reading, if you build or buy anything on these models, is that a liquid discount market is the most honest price signal the AI industry currently produces. It is doing the price discovery the vendors would rather not.

## A resale market is a diagnosis, not a hack

One commenter on the original piece shrugged that reselling something you got for free is not a clever trick. True, but it misses what the trick reveals. Arbitrage at scale only exists when the official price sits above the price the marginal buyer will actually pay. A stable spread of 40 percent, holding across brokers, is the market reporting that a token is worth well under its sticker. Someone funds the gap: promotional credits, YC grants, free tiers, or outright theft. The discount is, roughly, the size of the subsidy.

So the rule of thumb is worth keeping. When something supposedly cheap grows a resale market, the cheapness is a subsidy, not a cost curve. I wrote earlier that [someone is subsidizing your AI compute](/2026/subsidized-ai-compute/) from the supply side, where chipmakers finance the customers who buy their chips. This is the same thumb on the scale, seen from the demand side. The credits changing hands at half price are the receipt.

## Free credits are money the labs did not mean to print

Labs handed out credits to win developers, which is a sensible way to buy adoption. The problem is what a credit is. It is fungible, it is transferable, and it holds value, which makes it a bearer instrument. Give away scrip and you have issued a currency you no longer control the price of. Airlines ring-fence miles and gift cards carry no-resale terms for exactly this reason; the labs skipped that step and are now discovering why it exists.

The second-order effect lands on anyone reading usage as a proxy for demand. The same trace estimates that abuse runs at 10 to 20 percent of certain providers' traffic. A slice of what looks like roaring AI adoption is arbitrage and resale, not a customer who needed the answer. If you are an investor extrapolating a usage curve, or a founder benchmarking against a competitor's reported volume, part of the line you are trusting is people flipping free tokens.

## Cheap tokens of unknown origin are a supply-chain decision

For a CTO the discount is a trap dressed as a bargain. Buying gray-market access means routing your prompts, and whatever sits in them, through a proxy that terminates the encrypted connection at its own server. The reseller can log everything, swap the model so you pay for Claude and get something distilled, or tamper with a tool call on the way through. You have no way to check any of it. This is the AI version of counterfeit hardware: the price is genuinely lower, and so is the assurance that you are getting what you think, handled the way you think.

That is a governance question, not a procurement one, and it connects to the harder problem underneath all of this. The scarce thing when building on these models was never cheap intelligence; it is [knowing whether the intelligence was right](/2026/your-model-was-never-your-moat/). A supply chain you cannot inspect makes that job impossible before you have written a line of your own product.

## The number on your plan is borrowed

Here is where it touches the business case. If your unit economics only clear at discounted token prices, whether the discount is one you are quietly enjoying or one your competitor is advertising, you do not have a product. You have a position in the compute market. Detection, incidentally, is not the hard part; matching cards, phone numbers and IP addresses catches most of this, and the labs will get around to it once cheap capacity stops being a growth story they want to tell. When they do, the free fuel drains and the real price surfaces.

That surfacing is a repricing event, and a lot of thin-margin AI businesses have never modeled it. The teams I would trust here already know their numbers at two or three times today's token price, the same discipline I keep coming back to about [running the math at the unsubsidized rate](/2026/subsidized-ai-compute/). The gray market is a preview of the bill after the subsidies stop, published early, in public, by people with money on the line. Worth reading now.
