John Carmack read a translation of Musashi's *Book of Five Rings*, noticed how swordsmanship drifted from battlefield necessity to sport and hobby, and [drew the parallel for programmers](https://x.com/ID_AA_Carmack/status/2098443262214230095): don't be the out-of-touch kung-fu master, heir to lifetimes of tradition, who gets mauled by an amateur MMA fighter. His narrow claim is that hand-writing code is turning from a skill you need into a craft you keep because you enjoy it. I think that's right. The part worth your attention is what it does to the people you trust and the things you reward.

The master doesn't lose that fight for being weak. He loses because he trained for a fight that changed, and nobody in his dojo told him.

## The proxy just broke

Every engineering org rewards a proxy. You can't measure judgment directly, so you reward the things that used to come with it: elegant code, deep systems knowledge, the ability to hold a whole service in your head and hand-write the tricky bit at 2am. For thirty years those were honest signals. Someone who could do them was almost always someone worth listening to.

The proxy and the thing it stood for have come apart. Much of the manual craft we treated as evidence of skill is now [the part a good model does first, which is why writing the code got cheap and reviewing it became the job](/2026/reviewing-is-the-job/). When a proxy breaks, the reward system keeps paying out on it anyway, because the people who embody the proxy are the same people who set the standards. That is the quiet failure. You are not just over-crediting a skill. You are over-crediting it through the judgment of the people it made famous.

## Two skills, one word

"Experience" is doing too much work. It bundles two different things that used to travel together and are now separating fast.

One is pattern-judgment: what's worth building, what will page you at 3am, which shortcut is fine and which one ends a company. The other is manual execution, actually writing the thing by hand. The first ages slowly and ports across tools. The second is depreciating in front of us.

Most senior reputations are a bundle of both, priced as one. So when execution loses value, the reputation doesn't reprice, and you keep deferring to a veteran on "how hard is this" long after that has become the question they are worst at. The move I'd make is to unbundle it on purpose. Keep listening to your veterans on *should we* and *what matters*. Stop treating them as the last word on *can we, and how fast*, unless they've touched the tools this quarter. Their role is drifting toward judgment and review anyway, the way [AI has turned every engineer into a manager of work rather than a doer of it](/2026/every-engineer-a-manager/).

## Closed excellence is fragile

We have run this experiment before, outside software. When the early UFC put traditional martial arts into open competition in 1993, a slight Brazilian named Royce Gracie beat larger men from far more revered styles. The styles weren't fake. They had spent decades optimizing for the dojo instead of the fight, producing beautiful form and untested effectiveness.

Most engineering teams have only ever competed inside their own conventions. The review is friendly, the standards are internal, the bar is set by the same people clearing it. That is a dojo. The open fight arrives anyway, in the shape of a competitor who ships your six-month roadmap in six weeks with three people and a stack of agents, and doesn't care that their code would embarrass your principal engineer. Effectiveness in the real fight is the only score that survives contact, and the durable edge is [how fast your team relearns](/2026/how-fast-your-company-learns/) when the rules move again.

## The part that stings if you run the place

The corollary is simple and unwelcome. If status in your company is tied to the craft that's depreciating, the people most likely to resist the new tools are your most senior people, and it isn't a character flaw. It's an incentive. You are asking someone to champion the thing that devalues the skill their authority is built on. A memo won't move that. Changing what you reward will.

None of this means hand the keys to whoever used an agent last week. The amateur wins the first fight and loses the tenth, because recency without judgment ships fast and breaks things you only see later. The people who dominate are the pragmatists who added the new tools to real fundamentals, the ones who train *and* fight. That pairing is what you are actually hiring and promoting for now.

So keep your masters, and keep them in the fight. A veteran who stops practicing keeps the authority and loses the calibration, which is the worst of both. Give them a thin thread of real, current work, not to do the team's job, just to keep their instincts honest. And audit, once, what your promotion packet actually rewards. If it still pays for hand-crafted elegance as a stand-in for skill, you are funding a hobby and calling it engineering.
