Anyone who creates a Withings account on or after October 13 needs a Withings+ subscription to sync data automatically to third-party apps, including Apple Health. [MacRumors reports](https://www.macrumors.com/2026/10/15/withings-plus-apple-health-syncing/) the price at $99.50 a year in the US. Accounts created earlier keep free sync, and so do their owners when they buy a new scale.

The company is entitled to charge for its product, and recurring costs are real. But look at what is being priced. A weight reading is a few bytes the customer generated by standing on a scale they paid for, and the paywall sits on the road out. That is a price on friction, and I think friction is getting much cheaper to remove.

## A connector paywall prices switching, not service

The honest defense is that, as users describe the setup, the scale talks over Wi-Fi to Withings' cloud before the data reaches your phone, and servers cost money. Fair. Then charge for the cloud, at a price that looks like the cost of moving a few readings a day. $99.50 a year looks like the price of being the only door.

The grandfathering tells you the same thing. Keying the rule to account creation date protects the existing customers who would complain loudest and the press that would amplify them. That is smart pricing design, and it also suggests the company expects the charge to be unpopular with exactly the people who know the product best.

## The cost of getting around a closed connector just collapsed

Christian Schaller [recently described](https://blogs.gnome.org/uraeus/2026/10/15/aiandreverseengineerhardware/) using Claude Code to reverse engineer hardware on Linux. He recorded the vendor's Windows driver talking to the real device, captured the USB traffic, did the same with his own driver, and let the model diff the two. A film scanner that had stalled for months produced a first working scan within hours of restarting this way. On a new ThinkPad's camera, he had kernel and libcamera patches by the end of one day. He is open about the limits: he still had to hold the hardware and judge the results.

A scale is a different device, and Withings sync reportedly runs through its cloud rather than a cable. Still, the pattern carries over. The skill of watching one system's behavior and rebuilding the other side used to be scarce and slow. It is now closer to a patient afternoon with a good agent. If your moat is that integrating with you is tedious and specialized, that moat is a depreciating asset, and the depreciation schedule just changed.

## Closing the door has a cost of its own

The usual corporate answer is to harden things: signed binaries, attestation, tighter terms. It works for a while. It also turns a pricing decision into an arms race, and the engineering hours you spend defending the paywall buy no value for any customer. Meanwhile your most technical users, the ones who recommend products to everyone else, spend their weekends on the other side of the fence.

There is also a buyer-side shift. People are about to stop wiring things together by hand. Their assistants will do it, as I wrote in [your next customer might be an agent](/2026/your-next-customer-is-an-agent/). An agent shopping on someone's behalf treats a paywalled connector as a defect and routes around it, or picks the competitor whose data flows out without a fee. One of the commenters on the Withings story said Apple Health export was their main reason for buying the device, and that they had already switched glucose monitors for the same reason.

## What I'd do differently

If I were advising a company with a charge like this, I would run a short audit.

- List every fee that sits on a pipe: API tiers, export charges, premium connectors, per-seat integration add-ons.
- For each, ask whether the price tracks value created or difficulty to replicate. Write down which one honestly.
- Assume the difficulty half shrinks every year, and see what revenue survives.

Revenue priced on value tends to hold up, as with sleep analysis a customer actually uses or a clinician report. Revenue priced on difficulty is a countdown, and the assistants customers already use are the clock. The same applies on the buying side: portability and export terms belong in the contract before you sign, not in a support ticket after. It is the same logic as [cheap to fork, costly to keep](/2026/cheap-to-fork-costly-to-keep/), and it is why [the model was never the moat](/2026/your-model-was-never-your-moat/) either.

I don't know whether Withings will widen the paywall to existing accounts, as some of its users expect. I do know that the more a business depends on the connection staying hard, the more exposed it is to how fast AI is making hard things easy.
