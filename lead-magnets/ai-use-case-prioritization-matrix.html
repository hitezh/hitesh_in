Worksheet for leadership teams

AI Use Case Prioritization Matrix and One-Page Business Case

A 90-minute working session for a leadership team that has more AI ideas than budget, ending with one use case to pilot, a list to buy, a list to drop, and a one-page business case for the winner.

Why this matrix has more than two axes

The usual matrix plots value against feasibility. It is a fine start, and it sends most teams to the wrong first project, because "feasible" gets judged from a demo. A demo shows that a model can produce the output once, with an expert steering. It tells you nothing about what it costs to trust that output ten thousand times.

Producing a candidate answer is now something you rent by the token. Deciding whether the answer is right is the constraint, and that part you have to build yourself. So this matrix keeps value and adds the criteria I find decide whether an AI project pays off:

  • how cheap the output is to check
  • what a wrong answer costs, and whether you can undo it
  • whether the task is hard or merely messy
  • whether a smaller model, or a product you can buy, would do the job
  • who owns the outcome
  • what it costs at production volume, with the checking included

Score tasks, never job titles. A role is a bundle of tasks that score very differently, and the org chart will mislead you. You will find automation candidates inside senior roles and stubbornly human work inside junior ones.

1. Run it as a 90-minute leadership session

Invite five to eight people: the heads of the functions that proposed candidates, someone from finance, someone from engineering or IT, and one person who does the day-to-day work in each candidate area. Print parts 2 to 6. The 1 to 3 scale, the gates and the timings in this document are a starting point, not a standard. Change them to suit your team.

Before the session

  • Each function head lists the workflows that consume the most hours in their function (aim for 20) and marks the three they would hand to AI first.
  • Every candidate is written as a task with a start and an end ("match supplier invoices to purchase orders"), never as a role ("automate accounts payable").
  • One intake form (part 2) is filled in for each candidate that will be discussed. Limit the session to eight.
  • Someone brings last quarter's volume for each candidate: how many times the task was done, and roughly how long each one took.

Notes on scoring honestly

  • B, cheap to check. A better model does not raise this score. A weak model produces obvious garbage that is cheap to reject. A strong one produces output that is plausible and wrong in ways that look right, which needs full scrutiny. Score the check you have today.
  • C, cost of being wrong. Count the steps. Errors compound: at 90% reliability per step, a five-step task finishes correctly about 59% of the time. A long chain that ends in an irreversible action is a 1, however good the model looks.
  • D, hard, not messy. Difficulty is the wrong test. A problem that is hard but self-contained with a crisp answer suits a model. A task a new hire learns in a month can stump it when the task is soaked in unwritten context. Score the mess, not the prestige.
  • F, duller tool. "Which LLM?" is the third or fourth question on a roadmap. Ask first what the decision is and how often it repeats. Capability you do not need on this task is a cost.
  • H, cost at volume. Price per token is the wrong number. Use the cost of one finished unit of work, and include the minutes of human judgment each unit still consumes.

Pilot first where checking is cheap

Your first pilot will probably be boring: a workflow where an invoice either matches or it doesn't. That is the point. A tight feedback loop can be tested in weeks and reduces uncertainty cheaply. Teams keep pointing AI at the task where it looks most impressive, and impressive and deployable are different axes.

Buy before you build

Most companies should buy before they build, and that includes buying the boring specialist. Building the first version was never the expensive part. Keeping it alive for five years is. Before you approve a build, answer these:

  • Is there a product that already does this task for companies like yours? If yes, what exactly would your own build do that it cannot?
  • If you build or fork, who reviews the changes every month, and is that person's time in the budget?
  • Is the thing you would own boring, slow-moving and well-scoped? Those are worth owning. Security-sensitive, fast-moving or regulated work is better bought.
  • Whichever you choose, will your test cases, your corrections and your review rules be stored somewhere you control, so you can change vendor or model later?
  • If you start with a frontier model as a fast first version, are you keeping every human correction as a labeled example? Those corrections are what later let a smaller, cheaper specialist take over the high-volume part.
  • Does the result live inside a tool your people already open every day? A standalone assistant has to win attention on its own, and usually loses.
Name the axis you will lose on. If two candidates survive and trade against each other, stop asking which is best. Say the trade aloud: "we will be slower so we can be cheaper", or "we will spend more so it feels instant". If nobody in the room can name the axis, the decision has not been made yet.

Name the budget the saving moves into. When a bill falls, the cost often moves into engineering time, testing effort or an expert's afternoon, where nothing measures it. If you cannot say who pays for the checking, you have moved the cost, and the saving on this page is fiction.

7. Pilot design checklist

A convincing pilot often measures the expert running it. Tick every item before the pilot starts, so the result tells you about the tool.

Who runs it

  • The pilot is run by a competent non-expert, the kind of person who will use the tool in production. Your strongest person is not on it.
  • After each task, the operator records one line: did the model do this, or did my prompting and correction do it?
  • You are counting the minutes of human judgment each finished unit still consumes.

What it is scored against

  • The test set is built from your own closed cases with known right answers: at least fifty.
  • "A good outcome" is written down in a sentence two reviewers would apply the same way.
  • You have a human baseline on the same cases: accuracy, time and cost.
  • The acceptance test was written before the build and is owned by someone who is not building or tuning.
  • Part of the test set is held back from whoever tunes the prompts, and from the vendor.
  • If a vendor is involved, the trial runs on your data. A demo on the vendor's data does not count as evidence.

What it measures

  • Cost per finished unit of work, with checking and rework included. Price per token is not reported.
  • The share of cases the model hands back to a human or refuses to decide, alongside how often it is right. A model that escalates everything looks safe and removes no work.
  • If people or customers interact with it live: how often they interrupt, abandon or stop responding.
  • Response time against the limit set by the person waiting, written down before the model was chosen.
  • Seats and prompts used are not a success measure. Usage is not an outcome.

How it is contained

  • The steps in the workflow are counted, and a human or hard guardrail sits before the first step that is expensive or permanent.
  • During the pilot the tool drafts, stages and recommends. It does not send, commit or execute.
  • Every human correction is saved as a labeled example somewhere you control.
  • The go or no-go test, the decision date and the decider are the ones written in the business case, unchanged.

8. Common ways the exercise goes wrong

  • You score job titles. "AI can do 40% of customer support" mixes exposure with evidence. Break the role into tasks and score those.
  • The most impressive candidate wins. The room falls for the demo of the strategy memo and ignores the reconciliation. Check column B again.
  • The model is chosen first and the task second. If the first slide names a model, send it back.
  • Feasibility is taken from a public benchmark. Benchmarks exist where grading is cheap, and most of your work is not on any of them. A vendor's score tells you about the benchmark's neighborhood. Only your own cases tell you about yours.
  • Savings are budgeted before checking. A team that automates generation and forgets verification has not cut cost. It has moved the risk where it cannot see it.
  • The business case uses the demo's cost. One run with a cheap prompt says little about ten thousand runs with retries, long context and a reviewer. Cost it per finished unit at full volume.
  • "Human in the loop" is treated as a safety answer. It is a pricing question. If the loop is a senior person fixing the model's mistakes, you have bought a power tool for experts. Price and staff it as one.
  • A dominated option gets equal airtime. It has a champion in the room, so the debate feels balanced. If it loses on every axis you care about, say so in the first five minutes.
  • Nobody owns the winner. Pilots that stall usually had no owner and no agreed test of success. Leave the room with both written down.
  • Everything on the list gets built. A fork you can generate for free is still a fork you own. Choose what to own by what you are willing to govern.
  • The session is run once. Models get cheaper and the trade-offs shift. A candidate you dropped this quarter may be worth a pilot next year, and one you built may now be worth buying. Rescore the list every six months.

If you finish the session with one owned pilot, a written test for it, and a short list of things you have decided not to do, the 90 minutes were well spent.
